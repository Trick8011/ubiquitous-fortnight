# Baroque Quick Facts — Trivia Cheat Sheet

Verified core facts for the most commonly asked trivia. Use to confirm dates, attributions, and locations before answering.

## Italian Baroque

- Caravaggio (Michelangelo Merisi da Caravaggio): 1571–1610. Invented the tenebrist style. Calling of Saint Matthew and Martyrdom of Saint Matthew (1599–1600, Contarelli Chapel, San Luigi dei Francesi, Rome — his breakthrough). Supper at Emmaus (1601, National Gallery, London), Bacchus and Medusa (Uffizi), Judith Beheading Holofernes (Palazzo Barberini), Death of the Virgin (Louvre — rejected for using a drowned woman as model, per legend). Killed Ranuccio Tomassoni in a duel in 1606, fled Rome under a death sentence; Malta, Sicily, Naples; died at Porto Ercole in 1610 awaiting a papal pardon. David with the Head of Goliath (Borghese) — Goliath's head is his self-portrait.
- Annibale Carracci: 1560–1609. Farnese Gallery ceiling (Loves of the Gods, begun 1597, Palazzo Farnese, Rome) — the classical counterweight to Caravaggio. With brother Agostino and cousin Ludovico founded the Bologna academy.
- Artemisia Gentileschi: 1593–c. 1656. Judith Slaying Holofernes (two versions: Naples, Capodimonte, c. 1612–13; Florence, Uffizi, c. 1620). First woman admitted to Florence's Accademia del Disegno (1616). Daughter of Orazio Gentileschi; raped by Agostino Tassi, testified under torture at the 1612 trial. Self-Portrait as the Allegory of Painting (Royal Collection).
- Gian Lorenzo Bernini: 1598–1680. Dominated Roman Baroque sculpture and architecture. Borghese marbles: Apollo and Daphne (1622–25), David (1623–24), Pluto and Proserpina — all Galleria Borghese, carved for Cardinal Scipione Borghese. Ecstasy of Saint Teresa (1647–52, Cornaro Chapel, Santa Maria della Vittoria, Rome). Baldacchino over the altar of St. Peter's (1624–33, bronze partly from the Pantheon portico, per tradition). St. Peter's Square colonnade (begun 1656). Fountain of the Four Rivers, Piazza Navona (1651) — rivers: Nile, Danube, Ganges, Río de la Plata. Patron: Urban VIII Barberini ("Your luck is great to see Maffeo Barberini pope").
- Francesco Borromini: 1599–1667. Bernini's great rival. San Carlo alle Quattro Fontane (San Carlino), Sant'Ivo alla Sapienza (corkscrew lantern), Sant'Agnese in Agone on Piazza Navona. Undulating walls, geometric plans. Died by suicide.
- Guido Reni: 1575–1642. Aurora ceiling fresco (1614, Casino Rospigliosi, Rome). Bolognese classicism.
- Pietro da Cortona: 1596–1669. Allegory of Divine Providence ceiling (1633–39, Palazzo Barberini) — Barberini bees everywhere. Also an architect: Santi Luca e Martina.
- Andrea Pozzo: 1642–1709. Apotheosis of Saint Ignatius ceiling (Sant'Ignazio, Rome) — the textbook quadratura illusion; a marked disc on the floor gives the perfect viewpoint. Painted a fake dome on canvas.
- Guercino: 1591–1666. Aurora ceiling (Casino Ludovisi). Et in Arcadia Ego (first version of the theme, before Poussin).
- Caravaggisti: followers of Caravaggio across Europe — Orazio Gentileschi, Ribera in Naples, the Utrecht Caravaggists (Gerrit van Honthorst "Gherardo delle Notti," Hendrick ter Brugghen), Georges de La Tour in Lorraine.

## Spanish Golden Age

- Diego Velázquez: 1599–1660. Born Seville, court painter to Philip IV from 1623. Las Meninas (1656, Prado) — Infanta Margarita, the painter at his easel, the king and queen in the mirror; the red cross of Santiago on his chest added later (legend says by the king). Surrender of Breda ("Las Lanzas," Prado). Portrait of Innocent X (1650, Galleria Doria Pamphilj, Rome — the pope allegedly called it "troppo vero," too true; inspired Francis Bacon's screaming popes). Rokeby Venus (National Gallery, London — slashed by suffragette Mary Richardson in 1914). Old Woman Frying Eggs (early Seville bodegón).
- José de Ribera ("Lo Spagnoletto"): 1591–1652. Spanish, worked in Naples. Gritty tenebrist martyrdoms.
- Francisco de Zurbarán: 1598–1664. Monks, martyrs, and austere still lifes — "the Spanish Caravaggio." Agnus Dei (Prado).
- Bartolomé Esteban Murillo: 1617–1682. Seville. Soft Immaculate Conceptions (painted the subject roughly two dozen times) and street urchins.

## Flemish Baroque

- Peter Paul Rubens: 1577–1640. Antwerp. Elevation of the Cross and Descent from the Cross (Antwerp Cathedral, c. 1610–14). Marie de' Medici cycle: 24 huge canvases (1622–25, now Louvre). Ran a large workshop; also a diplomat — knighted by both Philip IV of Spain and Charles I of England. Second wife Helena Fourment, married when she was 16 and he 53, model for many late works. Massacre of the Innocents sold for a record £49.5 million in 2002.
- Anthony van Dyck: 1599–1641. Rubens's star assistant; court painter to Charles I of England from 1632, knighted. Charles I at the Hunt (Louvre), triple portrait Charles I in Three Positions (Royal Collection). Defined English aristocratic portraiture for centuries.
- Jacob Jordaens: 1593–1678. Antwerp's leading painter after Rubens and van Dyck died. The King Drinks feast scenes.
- Clara Peeters: c. 1594–after 1657. Antwerp still-life pioneer; first woman given a solo show at the Prado (2016).

## Dutch Golden Age

- Rembrandt van Rijn: 1606–1669. Born Leiden, worked Amsterdam. The Night Watch (1642, Rijksmuseum) — real title: Militia Company of District II under Captain Frans Banninck Cocq; it's a day scene darkened by old varnish; trimmed on all sides in 1715. Anatomy Lesson of Dr. Nicolaes Tulp (1632, Mauritshuis). Around 80 self-portraits across paintings, etchings, drawings. Wife Saskia died 1642; bankruptcy 1656. The Jewish Bride, Syndics of the Drapers' Guild (Rijksmuseum), Return of the Prodigal Son (Hermitage). The Storm on the Sea of Galilee — his only seascape — stolen in the 1990 Isabella Stewart Gardner heist, still missing. Master etcher: Hundred Guilder Print.
- Johannes Vermeer: 1632–1675. Delft; only about 34–36 accepted paintings. Girl with a Pearl Earring (c. 1665, Mauritshuis — "Mona Lisa of the North"; a tronie, not a portrait). The Milkmaid (Rijksmuseum), View of Delft (Mauritshuis — Proust called it the most beautiful painting in the world), The Art of Painting (Vienna, Kunsthistorisches). Possible camera obscura use. Died in debt; obscure until rediscovered by Thoré-Bürger in the 1860s. The Concert — stolen in the 1990 Gardner heist. Forged famously by Han van Meegeren (fooled Göring).
- Frans Hals: c. 1582–1666. Haarlem. The Laughing Cavalier (1624, Wallace Collection, London — he's neither laughing nor a cavalier). Loose, slashing brushwork admired by the Impressionists. Civic guard group portraits.
- Judith Leyster: 1609–1660. Haarlem; one of the first women in the painters' guild there. Self-Portrait (Washington). Long misattributed to Hals.
- Jan Steen: c. 1626–1679. Chaotic households — "a Jan Steen household" is still a Dutch phrase for a mess.
- Pieter de Hooch: 1629–1684. Delft courtyards and interiors, Vermeer's closest rival.
- Jacob van Ruisdael: c. 1628–1682. Greatest Dutch landscapist. Windmill at Wijk.
- Willem Claesz. Heda and Pieter Claesz: monochrome banquet still lifes. Rachel Ruysch (1664–1750): flower still lifes, long international career.
- Context: the Dutch had no big church or royal patrons — an open art market for merchants; painters specialized (landscape, still life, genre, marine).

## French Baroque

- Nicolas Poussin: 1594–1665. French but worked almost entirely in Rome. Et in Arcadia Ego (c. 1638, Louvre). Classical, cerebral — the counter-model to Rubens (the "Poussinistes vs. Rubénistes" color-versus-line debate).
- Claude Lorrain: 1600–1682. Ideal golden-light landscapes with tiny mythological figures; kept the Liber Veritatis to foil forgers. Obsession of English collectors — inspired the "Claude glass."
- Georges de La Tour: 1593–1652. Lorraine. Candlelit nocturnes — Magdalene with the Smoking Flame, The Fortune Teller (Met), The Cheat with the Ace of Diamonds (Louvre). Forgotten until the 20th century.
- Louis Le Nain and brothers: peasant scenes.
- Charles Le Brun: 1619–1690. First painter to Louis XIV, dictator of French art through the Academy. Hall of Mirrors ceiling at Versailles.
- Versailles: Louis XIV's palace, transformed from a hunting lodge from 1661; architects Louis Le Vau then Jules Hardouin-Mansart (Hall of Mirrors, with Le Brun); gardens by André Le Nôtre. Court moved there 1682.
- Hyacinthe Rigaud: portrait of Louis XIV in coronation robes (1701, Louvre).

## Patrons & Power

- Counter-Reformation: the Council of Trent (ended 1563) pushed art to be clear, emotional, and persuasive — the engine of the Catholic Baroque.
- Pope Urban VIII (Maffeo Barberini, r. 1623–44): Bernini's great patron — Baldacchino, Palazzo Barberini. Barberini bees on everything.
- Cardinal Scipione Borghese: nephew of Paul V; collector of Caravaggio and patron of young Bernini — hence the Galleria Borghese.
- Pope Innocent X (Pamphilj): Piazza Navona, Four Rivers Fountain, Velázquez's portrait.
- Philip IV of Spain: Velázquez's patron for nearly 40 years; also bought Rubens.
- Charles I of England: greatest collector-king — van Dyck, Rubens (Banqueting House ceiling, Whitehall — he walked beneath it to his execution in 1649); collection sold off by Cromwell.
- Louis XIV, the Sun King (r. 1643–1715): Versailles, Le Brun, the Academy — art as state power.

## Techniques & Terms

- "Baroque": likely from Portuguese "barroco," an irregular pearl — originally an insult.
- Tenebrism: extreme dark-vs-light, figures spotlit out of blackness (Caravaggio, Ribera, La Tour).
- Chiaroscuro: light-dark modeling generally — broader than tenebrism.
- Quadratura: illusionistic ceiling architecture (Pozzo); di sotto in sù: "seen from below" foreshortening.
- Trompe l'oeil: "deceives the eye."
- Genre painting: scenes of everyday life (Dutch specialty).
- Still-life types: vanitas (skulls, snuffed candles, hourglasses — the vanity of earthly life; Latin from Ecclesiastes), memento mori ("remember you must die"), banketje (banquet piece), pronkstilleven (ostentatious still life).
- Bodegón: Spanish kitchen/tavern still life (early Velázquez, Zurbarán).
- Tronie: Dutch character head, not a commissioned portrait (Girl with a Pearl Earring).
- Impasto: thick paint (late Rembrandt).
- Etching: acid-bitten printmaking — Rembrandt its greatest master.
- Camera obscura: darkened-box projection device, debated aid of Vermeer.
- Baldacchino: monumental canopy over an altar (Bernini's, St. Peter's).
- Bel composto: Bernini's fusion of sculpture, architecture, and painting into one theatrical whole (Cornaro Chapel).
- Fêtes galantes: Watteau's dreamy outdoor parties — the hinge into Rococo (Pilgrimage to Cythera, 1717).

## Frequently Asked One-Offs

- The Night Watch is not a night scene — darkened varnish earned it the nickname. Attacked three times (knife 1911, knife 1975, acid 1990); restored each time.
- The 1990 Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum heist (Boston): thieves dressed as police took 13 works including Rembrandt's Storm on the Sea of Galilee and Vermeer's The Concert — still unsolved, empty frames still hang.
- Caravaggio's police record: assault, libel, weapons charges, and the 1606 killing of Ranuccio Tomassoni — reportedly over a tennis (pallacorda) wager or a woman.
- Bernini vs. Borromini: the defining rivalry of Roman Baroque architecture. The legend that Bernini's Rio de la Plata figure shields its eyes from Borromini's Sant'Agnese is false — the fountain was finished before the church facade was begun.
- Velázquez traveled to Italy twice; on the second trip he painted Innocent X and Juan de Pareja, his enslaved assistant, whom he freed in 1650. Juan de Pareja's portrait is in the Met.
- Rembrandt was never "Rembrandt van Rijn" in signature — he signed with just "Rembrandt," first name only, like Raphael and Titian.
- Vermeer's entire output could fit in one gallery; the 2023 Rijksmuseum show gathered 28 — the most ever in one place.
- Rubens painted with his hat on in his self-portraits and famously said versions of "I paint a large work with more courage than a small one."
- Girl with a Pearl Earring's "pearl" is likely painted as an illusion — possibly glass or imaginary; it hangs in the Mauritshuis, The Hague.
- Artemisia's Judith is routinely read as painted revenge on Tassi — popular in trivia, debated by scholars.
- Watteau's Pilgrimage to Cythera (1717) was his Academy reception piece — a new category was invented for it: fête galante. It marks the Baroque-to-Rococo turn.
