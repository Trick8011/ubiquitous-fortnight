# RIVER TOWNS — Narration (condensed cut, ~4 minutes)

Voice direction: warm, unhurried, mid-register. Documentary cadence — let sentences land;
pause a full beat at each section break.

---

## 1. Cold open (over main title card)

Along the last four miles of Pennsylvania, where the state runs out at the Delaware River, there is a string of towns built for work. Refinery towns. Shipyard towns. Marcus Hook. Trainer. Chester. Ridley. For more than half a century, these towns have also been something else: the home ground of one of the most feared outlaw motorcycle clubs in America. This is the story of the Pagans in Delco — where they came from, what they built, and why, after sixty years, they never really left.

## 2. Act I (over Act I card)

It began quietly. In 1959, in Prince George's County, Maryland, a man named Lou Dobkin founded a small riding club. Thirteen members. No one was afraid of them. By the mid-sixties, that had changed. The Pagans had joined the one percent — the outlaw clubs. Their patch became Surtur, the Norse fire giant. And beneath it, no rocker naming a territory. The Pagans didn't advertise where they were. If you needed to know, you knew. Through the early seventies, the club grew at a speed that alarmed police up and down the seaboard — and its center of gravity moved north, to Pennsylvania.

## 3. The map (over Delco map card)

To understand why the Pagans took root here, you have to understand what these towns were. Marcus Hook — an oil town since 1902. Chester — for a time, one of the great shipbuilding cities of America. A corridor of shift work, row houses, and corner bars, stitched together by Route 13 and I-95, with a state line minutes away in either direction. The bike culture of the lower Delaware wasn't imported. It grew here. And when the shipyard closed and the refineries cut back, the clubs were still there. In towns that were losing everything else, the patch was something that stayed.

## 4. Act II (over Act II card)

In the eighties, the product was methamphetamine. Philadelphia and its suburbs became the meth capital of the East Coast, and law enforcement came to regard the Pagans as central to it. The money brought the club into conflict with the city's other organization — the mob. Under Nicodemo Scarfo, the mob demanded a street tax from anyone earning on its turf. The Pagans refused to pay. What followed was an ugly, mostly hidden war — kidnappings, beatings, standoffs — ended not by a treaty, but by indictments. The club pulled inward. The chapters that endured were the old ones, the deep ones. The river towns held.

## 5. Act III (over Act III card)

Then, in 2018, the club began to grow again — deliberately, nationally. Members called it the Blue Wave. Within a few years there were chapters in nearly every state. And through all of it, investigators who track the club kept placing its national leadership — its Mother Club — in one place: Delaware County, Pennsylvania. The government came back too. Federal drug and racketeering cases. A decades-long member from Marcus Hook, arrested at seventy-three. And on a Sunday afternoon in March of 2024, a fight among current and former members outside a Wawa on Chester Pike in Ridley ended in gunfire — and a county, briefly, staring at something it usually manages not to see.

## 6. Coda (over timeline card, then title reprise)

The shipyard never came back. The refinery runs with a fraction of the men. But on Friday nights, on Chester Pike and Post Road, you can still hear them coming before you see them. The Pagans were born in Maryland. But they grew up here — in the river towns. And the river towns, for better and for worse, are still theirs to come home to.
